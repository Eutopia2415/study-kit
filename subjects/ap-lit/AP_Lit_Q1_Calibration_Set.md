## How to use

1. Open the year's **Q1 Sample Responses PDF** (links below). Each PDF has the prompt, then essays 1A, 1B, 1C, then a scoring commentary at the end.
2. Read the poem and prompt first. Write down your own one-line reading of the "complexity" the prompt asks about.
3. Read each sample essay. **Stop before the "Scoring Commentary" pages.** Cover them, or have the AI grader see only the essay text.
4. Score each essay on the three rows below and record it in the blank score table.
5. For each score, write one sentence of justification that cites a specific moment in the essay. For Row B, name the weakest commentary sentence you found.
6. Then scroll down to **ANSWER KEY** at the bottom of this file and compare.
7. Targets:
   - Row A: exact agreement (it is almost always 1 on these samples).
   - Row B: **within 1 point**. Exact agreement on at least 2 of 3 samples per set.
   - Row C: exact agreement. Over-awarding Row C is the most common rater error. Nationally, the mean Row C score was about 0.04 in 2025.
   - Total: within 1 point.
8. If you miss by 2+ on Row B, reread the commentary and find which level descriptor you misapplied. The usual reasons are mistaking device-naming for commentary (that scores 1-2), or forgetting that 3 needs a line of reasoning.

AI-grader tip: give the model the rubric below plus the essay text only, and ask for row scores with evidence before it writes a total. Never paste the commentary pages into the grader's context.

## Current rubric (2020-present, 6 points)

Paraphrased from the 2025 Set 1 scoring guidelines. The structure is the same for 2023-2025.

### Row A: Thesis (0-1)
- **0:** No defensible thesis. It restates the prompt, summarizes without a claim, or doesn't answer the prompt.
- **1:** A defensible interpretation of the poem that answers the specific prompt (e.g., the speaker's *complex* experience).

### Row B: Evidence and Commentary (0-4)
| Score | Evidence | Commentary | Typical look |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Restates thesis, repeats given info, or irrelevant | none | Off-prompt, or opinion with no text |
| 1 | Mostly general | Summarizes evidence without explaining how it supports the argument | Summary or description. Names devices with little or no explanation. |
| 2 | Some specific, relevant evidence | Explains how *some* evidence relates to the argument, but there is **no line of reasoning, or a faulty one** | Mixes specifics with broad generalities. Explanations are simplistic, inaccurate, or repetitive. Makes one point well, but the claims aren't connected. |
| 3 | Specific evidence supports **all claims in a line of reasoning** | Explains how *some* evidence supports the reasoning. Explains how **at least one** literary element/technique contributes to meaning. | Builds a line of reasoning from multiple claims. Some evidence isn't integrated or explained. |
| 4 | Specific evidence supports all claims in a line of reasoning | **Consistently** explains how evidence supports the reasoning. Explains how **multiple** elements/techniques contribute to meaning. | Every claim has adequate, clearly explained evidence. |

Notes: mechanical errors that interfere with communication block the 4th point. Repeated instances of one technique can count as "multiple" only if each instance adds meaning. The guidelines don't use the phrase "claims exceed evidence". The equivalent test is "evidence supports **all** claims" (3-4) and "adequate evidence that is clearly explained" (4).

### Row C: Sophistication (0-1)
Earned by sophistication of thought and/or a complex literary argument, through any one of these:
1. Identifying and exploring complexities or tensions in the poem
2. Situating the interpretation in a broader context
3. Accounting for alternative interpretations
4. A consistently vivid and persuasive style

Not earned by sweeping generalizations used as context, merely hinting at alternatives, a thematic statement that isn't sustained, oversimplifying the poem, or fancy vocabulary that doesn't strengthen the argument. Sophistication has to run through the argument. A single phrase doesn't earn it.

## Reader commentary (paraphrased)

### 2025 Set 1: "Monologue for Saint Louis"
- **1A (B4, C1):** Specific evidence is paired with commentary on imagery, juxtaposition, and metaphor, and each one is tied to the poem's meaning. Row C comes from "identifying and exploring the complexities in the poem," such as the "blue-black" vs. green color contrast.
- **1B (B3, C0):** The evidence and commentary are insightful, but only some of the commentary supports the line of reasoning, so it misses the 4th point. The closing "map" idea is "not sustained throughout the essay," so no Row C.
- **1C (B1, C0):** Mostly general evidence and summary that never explains how evidence supports the thesis. The commentary notes it "does not provide any specific examples." No complex argument.

### 2025 Set 2: "Two Guitars"
- **1A (B4, C1):** It consistently links specific evidence to a clear line of reasoning across juxtaposition, details, and personification. Row C comes from exploring the tension between pain and passion, plus a vivid, persuasive style.
- **1B (B3, C0):** Specific evidence on first-person narration and personification, but some commentary is vague. The imagery discussion drifts to the audience's feelings instead of the central claim. The style is vivid only in places, so no Row C.
- **1C (B2, C0):** Some specific evidence mixed with broad generalizations. Commentary is vague, simplistic, and repetitive, and no line of reasoning forms.

### 2024 Set 1: "To a Star Seen at Twilight"
- **1A (B4, C1):** Specific evidence supports a clear line of reasoning, with commentary on how multiple techniques shape meaning. Row C comes from exploring the complex star-speaker relationship through to a firm conclusion.
- **1B (B3, C0):** Paired evidence and commentary across several techniques, but some explanations are unclear and don't consistently serve the argument. The opening contextual claim is a sweeping generalization, which doesn't earn Row C.
- **1C (B2, C0):** Relevant examples, but the commentary is simplistic or inaccurate and no line of reasoning is established.

## Chief Reader report takeaways (Q1)

### 2025 Set 1 (mean 3.46/6; A 0.93, B 2.49, C 0.04)
- Commentary was the main separator. Many essays quoted accurately (e.g., "crumbling heap of rotting black sticks") but never said why McElroy chose those words or what they show about the speaker.
- Strong essays connected claims into a line of reasoning instead of listing separate observations.
- Syntax (missing punctuation, lowercase) was usually just mentioned. Higher scorers tied it to the speaker's state of mind.
- Row C was almost never earned. Essays that got it sustained a tension (past vs. present) or a broader context (e.g., race) all the way through.

### 2025 Set 2 (mean 3.58/6; A 0.94, B 2.60, C 0.04)
- Some students missed that the poem is a conversation between two guitars. That misreading capped the analysis.
- Syntax comments were often "a brief reference lacking analysis and interpretation."
- Many essays summarized each guitar's traits instead of comparing them. Comparison is what produces a line of reasoning.
- Accurate quotes not tied back to the thesis were common. That is the evidence-without-commentary failure.

### 2024 Set 1 (mean 3.53/6)
- Weak theses praised the star in general. Strong ones made a precise claim about the speaker (e.g., what "alone" means here).
- Strong essays used the poem's structure as their line of reasoning, including the turn at "But there the likeness ends."
- Students quoted whole stanzas but rarely analyzed single words. Contrasting "solitary," "companionless," and "Alone" was what high scorers did.
- Weak commentary stopped at the star. Strong commentary carried it back to what it reveals about the speaker and humanity.

### 2024 Set 2 (mean 3.23/6)
- Generic theses ("the setting changed") could fit any poem and stayed low.
- Misreadings appeared, e.g., reading "fire begins to burn" as climate change or arson instead of summer heat. Those are claims the text doesn't support.
- Devices were identified but their function went unexplained. Word-level sound contrasts were mostly ignored.
- Strong essays traced the shift across the poem and linked the second half to the first. Weak ones listed examples.

### 2023 Set 1 (mean 3.48/6)
- Naming personification and imagery wasn't enough. High scorers explained what a device does (e.g., how "the warmer air deceives" complicates the seasonal change).
- General theses ("change is unfortunate but inevitable") scored lower than theses that held the speaker's contradictory responses.
- Isolated observations vs. extended discussion of tensions separated mid from high scores.

### 2023 Set 2 (mean 3.32/6)
- Weak essays tagged devices (simile, symbol, repetition) without connecting them to the speaker's complex experience.
- A common ceiling: explaining one idea well (peace in isolation) without relating it to the poem's other contradictions. That is the classic Row B 2.
- High scorers traced a progression (loneliness to isolation to freedom) and explored paradoxes, such as barrenness becoming refuge.

### Cross-year pattern on "claims exceeding evidence"
None of the six Q1 reports uses that exact phrase, so treat this as an inference. The reports flag the same problem in other words: generic theses that fit any poem, misreadings the text doesn't support (2024-2), quotations left unexplained, and context claims made as sweeping generalizations (2024-1, 1B). In rubric terms, a claim without adequate, explained evidence blocks the 4. If several claims are like that, there's no line of reasoning, which caps the essay at 2.
