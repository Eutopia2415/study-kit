# Writing drills (10 minutes each)

One drill fits in the gap between a cold question and a timed essay, or stands alone in a review slot. Each names the SKILLS.md row it trains. The tutor picks the drill from the weakest row, gives the material, times it, and grades in two lines: what worked, the one sentence to fix. Logged in `LOG.md` under Drills.

## Claim ladder (Claim creation: thesis from the tension; defensible claim)
Material: a prompt and its poem or passage. Five minutes: write three different theses. Then pick the one a reader could most easily disagree with and write one sentence on why it's the one to argue. The tutor says which of the three a reader would score 1 on Row A and why the others fall to restatement or to "the author uses X, Y and Z".

## The tension sentence (Claim creation; Text analysis: speaker's mind)
Material: a poem. One sentence in the shape "The speaker wants / says / claims A, but B", with a quoted word on each side. No device names allowed. Three poems in ten minutes.

## Two-sentence commentary (Writing: Row B)
Material: one quoted line. Sentence 1 names the choice in the words (not the device's name: "the line stops at 'still'", not "enjambment"). Sentence 2 says what that does to the speaker's attitude, no larger than the line can carry. Four lines in ten minutes. The tutor checks each pair for the claim-exceeds-evidence pattern.

## Shrink the claim (Writing: Row B)
Material: three sentences from the learner's own past essays that claim more than the evidence quoted (the tutor pulls them from `essays/`). Rewrite each so it says only what the quoted words show. Then one sentence of what more evidence would be needed to earn the original claim.

## Prompt parts, ticked (Writing: answering the whole prompt)
Material: a prompt. Break it into its parts as a checklist (what to analyze, which elements, "complex", "as a whole", what the ending adds). Then read one of your own essays and tick which parts it answered. Thirty seconds per part.

## Verb swap (Voice: precise verbs)
Material: five sentences with "shows", "uses", "conveys", "portrays", "highlights". Rewrite each with a verb that says what actually happens in the text (the speaker *bargains*, the line *refuses* to end, the image *shrinks*). No "this shows that".

## Introduce without summary (Writing: introducing the text; Sophistication: the wider frame)
Material: a Q3 prompt. Two sentences that tell a reader who hasn't read the novel what they need, then a thesis that reaches past the book. No plot after the second sentence. The argument is yours; the novel is the vehicle.

## Register pass (Voice: sentences that sound like a person, in an academic register)
Material: one of your own paragraphs. Mark every chat-register word (lol, basically, kinda, super, "like" as filler) and every template opener ("This shows that", "In this quote"). Rewrite the paragraph with the same ideas in the same number of sentences.

## Tone pairs (Words: confusable tone words)
Material: six short quoted lines, each with two tone words that get confused (wry / bitter, wistful / nostalgic, resigned / defeated, tender / sentimental, ironic / sarcastic, detached / cold). Pick one, prove it with a word from the line. Then the tutor flips one word in the line and asks whether the tone word survives.

## Timed paragraph (Writing: Row B under time)
Material: a thesis the tutor supplies and one quoted passage. One body paragraph in ten minutes: claim, evidence, two sentences of commentary, a sentence that links back to the thesis. Graded on Row B alone.

## Two readings (Text analysis; Sophistication: an alternative reading answered)
Material: one poem or passage and two plausible readings of it (the tutor supplies one, the learner the other, or both from the learner). Five minutes each: what each reading explains, and which lines it can't account for. Then one sentence of judgment: which accounts for more, or a qualified synthesis if the text supports both; the tutor doesn't force a winner. The point is better reading and commentary. Row C is a possible result, not the goal: the rubric gives it only when the complexity is developed through the argument, and mentioning a second reading earns nothing on its own.
